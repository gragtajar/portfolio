What beer actually is

Definition. Four ingredients, one process, endless variation. India has a legal definition, which is a useful place for a beginner to start because it is precise.

FSSAI, Alcoholic Beverages Regulations 2018, reg. 4.1

"Beer is a fermented alcoholic beverage made from barley malt or other malted grains, sometimes with added adjuncts like wheat, maize, corn, rice or other cereal crops, and with hops or hop extracts to impart a bitter taste and flavour."

Unpack that and you get the whole craft. Malted grain supplies sugar and colour. Water is the medium and, less obviously, a flavour ingredient. Hops supply bitterness, aroma and preservation. Yeast eats the sugar, makes alcohol and CO₂, and, the part beginners always miss, creates a large share of the flavour itself.

Fermented, not distilled

Beer sits at the low end

Fermentation alone can only take alcohol so far. Beer typically lands between roughly 3% and 12% ABV. Spirits get past that by distilling, concentrating alcohol after fermentation. Beer never does.

In India the law draws the line hard: anything sold as "beer" must be 0.5% to 8.0% ABV. Above 8%, it is legally something else.

Grain choice is identity

Barley is the default, not the rule

Barley malt dominates because barley husks form a natural filter bed and barley is rich in the enzymes that convert starch to sugar. Wheat, oats, rye, maize and rice all appear, each changing body, haze and mouthfeel.

Indian regulation explicitly permits wheat, maize, corn and rice as adjuncts, which is why most mass-market Indian lager is not all-malt.

A distinction worth fixing early

Strength and bitterness are independent. A Belgian Dark Strong Ale is 8–12% ABV with only 20–35 IBU: very strong, barely bitter. An American Pale Ale is 4.5–6.2% with 30–50 IBU: weak, noticeably bitter. Beginners routinely read "strong" as "bitter". They are different axes, and the explorer below lets you see them separately.

Ale versus lager

The one split that organises everything. Almost every beer in the world falls on one side of this line. It is a difference of yeast species and temperature, and everything else follows from it.

Top-fermenting

Ale

Yeast: Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the same species as bread and wine yeast. Works warm, works fast, and rises to the top of the vessel during fermentation.

What you taste: yeast-derived fruit and spice. Banana and clove in a German Weissbier, pear and pepper in Belgian ales, soft stone fruit in English bitters. The yeast is a visible author of the flavour.

Ales: IPA, pale ale, stout, porter, wheat beer, all Belgian styles, bitter, barleywine.

Bottom-fermenting

Lager

Yeast: Saccharomyces pastorianus, a hybrid species that works cold, works slowly, and settles to the bottom. Its wild parent, S. eubayanus, was only identified in 2011.

What you taste: almost no yeast character at all. Lagering, meaning extended cold conditioning (German lagern, "to store"), strips out rough edges. Malt and hops have nowhere to hide.

Lagers: pilsner, helles, Märzen, bock, dunkel, schwarzbier, and nearly every mass-market beer on earth.

The instructive exceptions

Kölsch
Fermented warm with ale yeast, then cold-conditioned like a lager. Result: a pale, delicate beer that drinks like a lager but carries a faint ale fruitiness. Cologne, Germany.
Baltic Porter
Called a porter, historically an ale style, but fermented cold with lager yeast. 6.5–9.5% ABV, dark, smooth, dried-fruit rich. The name tells you nothing about the yeast.
Lambic & Gueuze
Neither. Spontaneously fermented by wild yeast and bacteria from the open air of Brussels' Senne Valley. Sour, funky, and at 0–10 IBU essentially unbittered.

Takeaway: style names are historical accidents, not a taxonomy. The yeast is the real fact.

The four ingredients, and what each does to the glass

Composition. Read this as a drinker: the question is never "how is it made", it is "why does it taste like that".

Ingredient one

Water, the invisible ingredient

Beer is overwhelmingly water, and its dissolved minerals change what the other ingredients taste like. This is not a subtlety. It is why entire styles exist only where they do.

  • Burton-on-Trent, England: very high sulphate. Sulphate sharpens and dries hop bitterness, which is why Burton became the pale ale and IPA capital. Brewers elsewhere still add gypsum to imitate it, a practice called burtonisation.
  • Plzeň, Bohemia: exceptionally soft water. Soft water lets a heavy hopping rate stay round rather than harsh, which is half the reason Pilsner Urquell tastes the way it does.

Ingredient two

Malt: colour, sweetness, body

Grain is steeped, allowed to germinate, then dried in a kiln. How hot and how long that kiln runs decides the colour and much of the flavour. The spectrum runs continuously:

  • Palest kilning gives bready, cracker-like, neutral. The base of pilsners and pale lagers.
  • Medium kilning gives toast, biscuit, bread crust. Munich and Vienna malts, the backbone of Märzen and dunkel.
  • Caramelised gives toffee, caramel, raisin. Crystal malts, which give bitters and ambers their sweetness.
  • Roasted gives coffee, dark chocolate, char. Chocolate malt and roasted barley, what makes a stout black and bitter in a way hops never could.

A documented turning point

Before the hydrometer arrived around 1770, brewers could not measure yield. Once they could, they found brown malt produced 54 lb of extract per quarter against pale malt's 80 lb. Brewers switched to pale malt as the base for every beer and added small amounts of dark malt purely for colour. Daniel Wheeler's drum roaster of 1817 then created patent black malt, making that approach practical. Modern dark beer is a consequence of an accounting insight.

Ingredient three

Hops: bitterness, aroma, preservation

Hops are the cone-shaped flower of a climbing vine. They do two jobs that pull in opposite directions, and when the brewer adds them decides which job they do.

  • Added early, boiled long: the resins isomerise and you get bitterness, but the aroma boils away.
  • Added late, or after fermentation (dry-hopping): aroma and flavour with almost no added bitterness. This is why a Hazy IPA can smell enormous and taste comparatively soft, at 25–60 IBU against a classic American IPA's 40–70.

Hops also preserve. That property is why they displaced the older herb mixture gruit across medieval Europe: unhopped beer spoiled too fast to export.

Named varieties carry recognisable character. Saaz defines Czech pilsner's floral-spicy note; Cascade and Chinook carried the American craft movement's citrus-and-pine signature.

Ingredient four

Yeast, the hidden author

Yeast converts sugar into alcohol and CO₂. It also manufactures aroma compounds, and in ales those compounds are often the dominant flavour in the glass.

  • Esters give fruit. The banana in a Weissbier is not an additive, it is an ester the yeast made.
  • Phenols give spice. The clove alongside that banana, and the pepper in a Saison.
  • Wild organisms (Brettanomyces, Lactobacillus, Pediococcus) produce the funk and acidity of lambic, gueuze and Flanders reds.

Two names to know: Louis Pasteur, whose 1857 work established that yeast drives fermentation, and Emil Christian Hansen, who at the Carlsberg Laboratory in 1883 first isolated a pure yeast cell. Every lager yeast in commercial use descends from Hansen's isolate.

Reading the numbers on a tap list

Units. Four abbreviations do almost all the work. The ranges quoted here are derived from the 66-style dataset below, so they describe the real span of beer rather than a textbook ideal.

ABV Alcohol by volume
What share of the liquid is pure alcohol. A 5% beer is 5% ethanol by volume. Across the styles below it runs from 2.8% (American Light Lager, Berliner Weisse) to 14% (Eisbock). In India, beer is legally capped at 8.0%.
IBU International Bitterness Units
A laboratory measure of bitter compounds. Across these styles it runs from 0 (Lambic, Gueuze) to 100 (Double IPA, American Barleywine). Critically, IBU is not perceived bitterness, because sweetness masks it. An Imperial Stout at 50–90 IBU tastes far less bitter than an American Pale Ale at 30–50, because the stout has enormous residual malt sweetness to balance against.
SRM Standard Reference Method
Colour, as a number. Low is pale, high is dark. In this dataset 2 is palest straw (American Light Lager, Berliner Weisse), 10–17 is amber to copper, and 40 is effectively black (Irish Stout, Imperial Stout). Europe uses EBC instead, which is roughly double the SRM figure.
OG / FG Original & final gravity
Sugar density before and after fermentation, written as 1.048, 1.012 and so on. The gap between them is what the yeast ate, so it predicts alcohol; how high the FG lands predicts body and sweetness. Czech Premium Pale Lager runs OG 1.044–1.060 down to FG 1.013–1.017.
°P Degrees Plato
The same sugar measurement on a different scale, standard in continental Europe. This is the most common beginner trap. A Czech beer labelled "12°" is 12 degrees Plato, a measure of sugar density, not strength. That beer is around 5% ABV, not 12%. Czech pubs label beers 10°, 11°, 12° routinely.

Honest limitation

The ranges above are derived from the verified 66-style dataset in this guide, not from a fetched metrological standard. The formal definitions (IBU as milligrams per litre of isomerised alpha acid, the exact SRM-to-EBC conversion, the Plato-to-gravity formula, and the legal volumes of an imperial versus US pint) could not be sourced and are queued for a later pass.

Where to start, in eight rungs

The route in. Every rung introduces exactly one new idea, and each is chosen so its numbers make the lesson obvious. Climb in order and you will understand beer by tasting rather than by reading.

How this ladder was built

The specifications on each rung are verified BJCP figures. The ordering itself is my editorial recommendation, reasoned from those specifications, not a ranking published by any authority. The logic: hold alcohol low early, introduce one variable at a time, and delay bitterness until after you have met malt, yeast and roast, because bitterness is the trait beginners most often mistake for "beer I dislike".

0

Where you already are: International Pale Lager

4.5–6.0% ABV · 18–25 IBU · SRM 2–6

Kingfisher, Budweiser, Heineken, Corona, Tuborg. This is the baseline, not an insult. Know its numbers so every later rung has something to measure against: mild bitterness, pale colour, minimal yeast character.

1

The same thing, done properly: Munich Helles or German Pils

Helles 4.7–5.4% · 16–22 IBU  |  Pils 4.4–5.2% · 22–40 IBU

Identical family, identical strength, far more flavour. Helles shows you what malt tastes like when nothing is hiding it. German Pils, at up to 40 IBU, shows you what hop bitterness feels like in a beer you already understand. Lesson: flavour is not a function of strength.

2

Meet yeast: Weissbier or Witbier

Weissbier 4.3–5.6% · 8–15 IBU  |  Witbier 4.5–5.5% · 8–20 IBU

Among the least bitter beers in the entire guide, yet enormously flavourful: banana and clove in the German, coriander and orange peel in the Belgian. This is the rung for anyone who thinks they dislike beer. If the problem was bitterness, these solve it at a stroke.

3

Meet malt and break the colour myth: Munich Dunkel or Vienna Lager

Dunkel 4.5–5.6% · 18–28 IBU · SRM 17–28  |  Vienna 4.7–5.5% · 18–30 IBU · SRM 9–15

Visibly dark, no stronger than a Kingfisher, and no more bitter. Toasted bread crust, nuts, light chocolate. Lesson: dark does not mean strong, heavy or bitter. That single correction unlocks half the beer world.

4

Meet roast: Irish Stout

3.8–5.0% ABV · 25–45 IBU · SRM 25–40

Black as it gets, and weaker than most Indian lagers. Coffee and dark chocolate come from roasted grain, not from hops or alcohol. On a nitro pour it is also your introduction to how gas changes texture. Lesson: roast is a fourth flavour axis, separate from malt sweetness, hops and yeast.

5

Meet hops properly: American Pale Ale, then American IPA

APA 4.5–6.2% · 30–50 IBU  |  IPA 5.5–7.5% · 40–70 IBU

Do these in this order, and only now. Citrus, pine and resin are hop flavours, distinct from hop bitterness. If the IPA is too much, try a Hazy IPA (6–9%, 25–60 IBU): same aroma intensity, markedly less bite, because the hops went in late rather than early.

6

Meet Belgian yeast: Dubbel, then Tripel

Dubbel 6.0–7.6% · 15–25 IBU  |  Tripel 7.5–9.5% · 20–40 IBU

Now the alcohol climbs, and the point is how well it hides. A Tripel at 9.5% with only 20–40 IBU tastes of spice and citrus rather than heat. Lesson: strength is something a brewer can conceal, which is precisely why it is worth counting your standard drinks.

7

The frontier: Gose, then Imperial Stout or Barleywine

Gose 4.2–4.8% · 5–12 IBU  |  Imperial Stout 8–12% · 50–90 IBU

Gose is the gentlest possible door into sour beer: low alcohol, nearly no bitterness, tart and faintly salty. At the other extreme, Imperial Stout and Barleywine are sipping beers where every axis is turned up at once. If these still read as "beer", you have arrived.

Two shortcuts

If you dislike bitterness, go 0 → 2 → 3 → 6 and skip rung 5 entirely. Wheat beers, dunkels and Belgian dubbels are a complete and satisfying path that never exceeds about 28 IBU.

If you find beer boring, go 0 → 4 → 5 → 7. Roast and hops are the fastest routes to intensity.

Temperature, glass, pour and food

Handling. Most bad beer is mishandled rather than badly brewed. Four variables account for nearly all of it, and one of them matters enormously if you eat Indian food.

Temperature

The Certified Cicerone syllabus states the band plainly: beer "should be served between 38 and 55 °F (3–13 °C) depending upon its style", and "reveals more flavor as its temperature increases".

There is no style-by-style temperature table in any authoritative source retrieved for this guide, so none is invented here. What exists is the governing rule, from the Brewers Association: "Assume that the higher the ABV, the warmer the serving temperature." Around 45–50 °F (7–10 °C) is described as common for many styles.

Why ice-cold hurts

Cold is an anaesthetic

Beer served near freezing "blinds the taste experience", with taste buds numbed and a bland result. It also retains more CO₂, which makes the beer more filling. This is exactly why mass-market lager is served so cold: at 2 °C you cannot taste much, which is fine when there is not much to taste.

Frosted glasses are a mistake

Never use a freezer glass

Both the Brewers Association and Cicerone advise against frosted glassware. An ice sheet on the glass triggers rapid CO₂ release and excessive foaming, chills the beer below its style range, and can carry frozen sanitiser or freezer odours into the drink. Room-temperature or chilled-but-dry is correct. The Brewers Association notes room-temperature glassware is preferred for most craft styles, with chilled glass acceptable for standard lagers.

The 3-30-300 rule

Cited by the Brewers Association from a large brewery study: the same amount of flavour loss results from 3 days at 90 °F, 30 days at room temperature (72 °F), or 300 days at 38 °F. Heat is time. A crate left in an Indian summer boot for an afternoon has aged meaningfully.

The mechanisms, in the chart's own terms: a stem or handle keeps body heat away from the beer; an outward-curved upper shape traps and concentrates aroma; an inward-curving shape concentrates foam; a large bowl invites you to cup and deliberately warm a strong beer.

Not sourced, so not claimed

Teku, stange and goblet/chalice appear in no source retrieved for this guide, and neither does laser-etched nucleation. They may well be legitimate; this guide simply has no basis to describe them.

The pour

Standard CO₂ beer

Forty-five degrees, then straight

  • Hold the glass at 45°, about an inch below the tap. Never let the tap touch the beer or foam.
  • Open the tap fully and fast. A half-open tap causes turbulence, excess foam and waste.
  • Pour down the side until half full, then tilt upright and pour down the middle.
  • Finish with a one-inch collar of foam. German wheat beers and Belgian ales traditionally take 2–3 inches.

That head is not decoration: it releases aromatic volatiles and enhances the palate-cleansing effect of carbonation.

Nitro beer

Why Guinness is different

Nitrogenated beer carries much lower dissolved CO₂ (1.1–1.7 volumes) plus nitrogen, and is dispensed through a 75% nitrogen / 25% CO₂ blend and a restrictor plate that forces the beer through tiny holes. That turbulence produces the cascade.

Nitrogen bubbles are smaller than CO₂ bubbles, which creates the creamy mouthfeel and a markedly more stable, longer-lasting head. The trade-off, stated by the Brewers Association: nitro will subdue aroma and soften the perception of bitterness.

The two-part pour is heritage: it descends from Guinness once being blended from two casks, one aged and one fresh. Today it controls the head.

Beer and Indian food

The standard framework has three principles: complement (shared flavours resonate), contrast (an opposing flavour highlights the dish), and cut (carbonation, sourness and bitterness lift rich or fatty flavours off the palate). Before any of those, match intensities so neither side overwhelms the other.

Then there is chilli, and here the Cicerone syllabus is unusually specific. It lists which beer traits act on capsaicin heat, and the answer is counterintuitive:

Soothe the heat

  • Malt flavours soothe and soften capsaicin heat
  • Sweetness soothes capsaicin heat and other spices

Amplify the heat

  • Carbonation accentuates capsaicin heat
  • Bitterness accentuates capsaicin heat
  • Alcohol can accentuate capsaicin heat

What that implies, stated as synthesis rather than as a sourced pairing

Reading those five rules together: with a genuinely hot dish, reach for something malty, slightly sweet, moderately carbonated and low in bitterness. A Munich Dunkel, a Sweet Stout or a Dunkles Weissbier fit. The IPA is the worst possible choice, because it is high on all three amplifiers at once: bitterness, carbonation and alcohol. The ice-cold strong lager that is the Indian default is working against you on two of the three.

One sourced pairing points the same way: the syllabus's own example is that "an Indian curry with cloves resonates with the clove flavors found in a Dunkles Weissbier", because yeast-derived phenols complement spices while contrasting fat and umami. That is the single most useful pairing in this guide for Indian cooking.

Foam, lacing and freshness

What lacing actually tells you

It is a glass test, not a beer test

If the glass is clean, foam adheres in parallel rings after each sip. If it is not, foam adheres randomly or not at all. Likewise, bubbles clinging to the inside of the glass in the liquid mean the glass is not clean.

Grease is the enemy. Washing beer glasses alongside food dishes transfers grease that destroys foam, as do fat- or oil-based detergents, and dairy residue from coffee mugs in a shared washer. Air-dry inverted; towel-drying leaves lint.

How long it keeps

Freshness, by package

  • Non-pasteurised draught: 45–60 days
  • Pasteurised draught: 90–120 days
  • Bottled or canned: up to 6 months
  • Hoppy styles such as IPA: changes in as little as 3 months, even refrigerated

Beer must never exceed 25 °C, which causes rapid degradation. Only strong, sour and smoked beers age well. Hoppy beers are the clear drink-fresh category.

Light damage, ranked

Skunking is caused by sunlight, fluorescent light and most LED light, and may be evident after just a couple of minutes of exposure. Protection, best to worst: cans and closed case boxes (maximum), brown glass (blocks most of the damaging wavelengths), green glass (blocks very little), clear glass (no protection at all). A beer sitting under a lit fridge display in a clear bottle is being damaged while you look at it.

Reading an Indian beer label

India, the law. India regulates beer labels in unusual detail, and several of those rules explain things you have probably noticed without understanding, including why the ABV is so often a band rather than a number.

  1. The statutory warning. Mandated in two lines by reg. 5.12, and it must sit inside a box. Minimum text height is 1.5 mm on packs up to 200 ml and 3 mm above that. The odd punctuation of "Be safe-dont drink and drive" is how the regulation itself prints it.
  2. Alcohol content. Must be expressed as "% abv" or as proof. But for beer specifically the label may state a band instead of a figure: "upto 5%" for regular, "above 5% upto 8%" for strong. That is why so many Indian beers never tell you the actual number. Legal tolerance is ±0.3%.
  3. Expiry, not "best before". For any alcoholic beverage under 10% alcohol, which is all beer, the label must carry "Expiry date" or "Use by" with date, month and year. "Best before" is permitted only as additional information, never as the required declaration.
  4. No green or brown dot. The veg / non-veg mark used on Indian food packaging is explicitly disapplied to alcoholic beverages. The one exception anywhere in the rules is wine containing egg-white or isinglass residue.
  5. No health claims, and almost no nutrition panel. Health claims are banned outright. Nutritional information is excluded too, with a single carve-out: energy content in kcal, which a brand may declare voluntarily.
  6. FSSAI licence number alongside the FSSAI logo: the brand owner's, plus the bottler's or importer's if different. Net quantity follows the Legal Metrology Act, not FSSAI.

FSSAI also sets hard limits for beer sold in India: pH 3.3–4.8, CO₂ 1.8–3.6 volumes, methanol max 50 mg/l, lead max 0.2 mg/l. Beer must be clear, wheat beer excepted, and bottled or canned beer must be pasteurised or filter-sterilised. Draught beer is exempt from that last rule, which is precisely why draught at a microbrewery can taste fresher and fuller than the same beer from a bottle.

How much are you actually drinking?

India has an official standard drink, defined in the beer regulation itself: one standard drink contains 12.7 ml of alcohol by volume at 20 °C (reg. 5.2). Printing the count on the label is voluntary, so you will rarely see it, which makes it worth computing yourself.

Formula: (volume × ABV ÷ 100) ÷ 12.7. Derived arithmetic from FSSAI's definition. The regulation gives its own worked example of a 750 ml beverage at 8%, stated as "approximately 4.8 standard drinks". The tier shown is the FSSAI band, not your state's excise band.

Driving: Motor Vehicles Act 1988, Section 185

The legal limit in India is 30 mg of alcohol per 100 ml of blood, among the stricter limits worldwide. A first offence carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of ₹10,000, or both; a repeat offence up to two years or ₹15,000, or both. Since a single 650 ml strong beer is over four standard drinks, the practical reading is simple: if you have had a beer, do not drive.

What is actually in the fridge

India, the market. Four companies account for most of what you can buy in India. Here is who owns what, and one finding that matters more than the roster itself.

The finding

Not one Indian brand owner publishes ABV figures on any source that could be reached for this guide. United Breweries' 179-page Integrated Annual Report 2025-26 contains the string "ABV" zero times. Carlsberg India's product pages give beer type, country of origin and launch year, but no strength. Simba and Mohan Meakin list beers without figures.

The practical consequence: the physical label is the only place strength appears, and the label is legally allowed to show a band rather than a number. If you want to know what you are drinking, you must read the bottle, and even then you may only get "above 5% upto 8%". The other citable route would be a state excise department's brand-registration schedule, which lists declared v/v per brand.

Microbreweries, as a state actually regulates them

Haryana's Excise Policy 2025-27 is a concrete example of the licensing regime behind every brewpub you drink at. A microbrewery licence is form L-10C, and it is restrictive:

  • Granted only to existing hotel or restaurant licence holders (L-4 / L-5)
  • Beer produced must not exceed 8% v/v
  • On-site consumption only. The licensee may not sell it for off-premises drinking, which is why you cannot buy a growler to take home
  • Licence fee ₹30 lakh in Gurugram, ₹20 lakh in Faridabad, Panchkula and Sonepat, ₹10 lakh elsewhere, plus ₹2 lakh security
  • Excise duty ₹40 per bulk litre on installed fermenting-tank capacity
  • Every batch certified fit by a qualified chemist; monthly government lab sampling

The policy's stated rationale is "to promote healthy drinking habit of liquor with low alcoholic content". United Breweries describes craft and microbrewery segments as "relatively small" but "influencing consumer preferences around taste, freshness, and experience".

The one number everyone quotes, and why it is not here

India's per-capita beer consumption is repeatedly described as low, but no sourced figure could be retrieved, and none is invented here. Carlsberg's and Heineken's 2025 annual reports contain the phrase "per capita" zero times. United Breweries states only that consumption is "significantly below global averages" and the market is "underpenetrated". The one hard adjacent number it does give: nearly 23 million Indians reach legal drinking age each year.

Likewise, the widely repeated claim that Indian volume skews heavily to strong beer could not be sourced as a percentage. The directional evidence is real (United Breweries calls the market "anchored by the strong and mild beer segments", AB InBev's Haywards is "India's original strong beer", Carlsberg's biggest India brand is Tuborg Strong) but no share figure is asserted.

Tasting, and what "off" tastes like

Sensory. Professional judging follows a fixed order. Learning it gives you language for things you already perceive but cannot name.

1

Appearance

Colour, clarity, head size, head retention, lacing down the glass. Use a consistent background.

2

Aroma

Swirl and sniff. Most of what you call "taste" is smell. Malt, hop and yeast character separate out here first.

3

Flavour

Let it reach the whole mouth. Identify malt, hop, yeast, and the balance between sweetness and bitterness.

4

Mouthfeel

Body, carbonation, warmth, creaminess, and astringency, which is a mouthfeel rather than a flavour.

5

Finish

What happens after you swallow. Dry or sweet, short or lingering. Perception continues past the swallow.

How it is weighted

The BJCP 50 points

Aroma 12 · Flavour 20 · Mouthfeel 5 · Appearance 3 · Overall 10

Flavour and aroma carry two-thirds of the score. Appearance carries almost none.

Building vocabulary, one rung at a time

A short history, with the myths marked

Origins. Beer may predate agriculture. It certainly predates most of what we think we know about it, including the story of the IPA.

c. 11,000 BCE

Raqefet Cave, Israel

Stone mortars in a Natufian burial cave, dated 13,700–11,700 years before present, held 121 starch granules from wheat or barley, oat and legumes. The excavators concluded Natufians stored malted seeds and made beer as part of mortuary ritual, several millennia before cereals were domesticated. The authors note the wider "beer drove agriculture" hypothesis remains controversial.

c. 3500 BCE

Godin Tepe, Iran

Jug fragments coated in beerstone give the earliest chemically confirmed barley beer.

c. 3000 BCE

Beer as wages

Workers at Uruk were paid in beer. Labourers on the Great Pyramid at Giza received four to five litres daily. In 2021 a 5,000-year-old beer factory was excavated at Abydos, dated to the reign of King Narmer.

c. 1800 BCE

The Hymn to Ninkasi

A 3,900-year-old Sumerian poem to the goddess of brewing contains the oldest surviving beer recipe, beer made from barley by way of bread. Clay tablets indicate most Mesopotamian brewers were women, and brewing was the only profession under divine protection.

822 CE

Hops enter the record

Abbot Adalard of Corbie writes about hops in beer. Adoption was slow but decisive: unhopped gruit beer spoiled too fast to export, and the alternative, raising alcohol, was expensive. Hops won on economics, not taste.

1516

The Reinheitsgebot

Duke William IV adopts the Bavarian purity law on 23 April. It restricted ingredients to water, barley and hops (yeast is absent, as brewers treated it as process rather than ingredient) and also fixed prices and capped innkeepers' profits. One motive was keeping wheat and rye for bakers so bread stayed affordable.

"Unchanged since 1516" is false. Bavaria allowed coriander and bay leaf by the mid-1500s; wheat beer survived only via an exclusive family privilege. A 1987 European Court ruling struck it down for imported beer, and today it functions essentially as a labelling standard.

1760–1817

Measurement changes everything

Thermometer (1760), hydrometer (1770), improved steam engines (1765). Brewers could finally measure, and pale malt's superior yield reorganised the industry. Wheeler's drum roaster (1817) produced patent black malt.

1842

Plzeň invents golden beer

Bavarian brewer Josef Groll brewed the first batch in Plzeň on 5 October 1842; it was first served on 11 November. Soft Plzeň water, English-style indirectly kilned pale malt and Saaz hops produced a beer that was golden, then a novelty. In Bohemia, lager breweries went from 32.5% of all breweries in 1860 to 97.9% by 1870.

1870

Refrigeration

Carl von Linde built the first large-scale refrigerated lagering tanks for Sedlmayr's Spaten brewery in Munich. Before that, brewers dug ice cellars and planted chestnut trees for shade above them, which is literally where the beer garden comes from.

1883

Pure yeast

Emil Christian Hansen isolates the first pure yeast cell at the Carlsberg Laboratory. All commercial lager yeast descends from it.

1855

Beer arrives in India, commercially

Edward Dyer set up a brewery at Kasauli in the Himalayas, incorporated as Dyer Breweries in 1855 and described as Asia's first. Its brand Lion was originally an India Pale Ale and became a lager only in the 1960s. It led the Indian market from the 1840s to the 1960s, succeeded by Golden Eagle, then Kingfisher from the 1980s. H. G. Meakin bought the Shimla and Solan breweries in 1887; the businesses merged after the First World War and became Mohan Meakin in 1967.

1978–1999

Kingfisher takes over

From United Breweries' own milestones: Kingfisher Premium launched 1978, canned 1981, exported to the UK 1982. Kingfisher Strong launched in 1999 and "soon becomes India's No.1 beer by volume", a fact that tells you a great deal about which way Indian taste ran. Ultra followed in 2009, Ultra Witbier in 2019.

1919–1933

American Prohibition

The Eighteenth Amendment was ratified on 16 January 1919 and Prohibition began in 1920, destroying most American breweries. The Twenty-first Amendment repealed it on 5 December 1933, the only amendment to repeal another.

1971–1980

The craft revival

CAMRA was founded on 16 March 1971, in a bar in County Kerry, Ireland, not England, and reached 5,000 members by 1973. In the US, Fritz Maytag's 1965 rescue of Anchor is the usual turning point; New Albion followed in 1976, homebrewing was legalised in 1978, and Sierra Nevada brewed its first Pale Ale in November 1980. Today an estimated over 40% of US craft beer is IPA.

The IPA myth, handled carefully

You will be told IPA was invented strong and heavily hopped so it could survive the sea voyage to India. Beer historians dispute this. What is documented: George Hodgson's Bow Brewery dominated early exports to India partly because it sat two miles from the East India Docks and offered 18 months' credit. What he shipped included an existing style, his old ale, which "benefited exceptionally from conditions of the voyage". Early IPAs were only slightly stronger than other beers of the period. And porter, not an IPA, was also shipped successfully to India and California.

Burton brewers entered the trade after the Napoleonic blockade cost them their European market; Allsopp's maltster Job Goodhead developed a pale ale in Hodgson's style, and Burton's high-sulphate water did the rest. Domestic English demand for the style only developed around 1840. The sceptical literature is Martyn Cornell's Amber, Gold & Black, Ron Pattinson's research, and Alan Pryor's "Indian Pale Ale: an Icon of Empire".
